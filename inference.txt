Experiment 4: Implementation of Channel Estimation and Equalisation
Result and Inference:
In this experiment, we studied the performance of channel estimation and equalization using LabVIEW and NI-USRP. The least squares technique is the simplest, and an added advantage of the least squares estimator is that in AWGN, the least squares estimator is also the maximum likelihood estimator. Generally, choosing the length of the training sequence much larger than the length of the channel gives better performance, and the full rank condition can be guaranteed by ensuring that the training sequence is persistently exciting and that it looks random enough. Random training sequences perform well while the constant training sequence fails. Furthermore, it is observed that training sequences with good correlation properties generally satisfy this requirement.

Experiment 7: Channel Coding in OFDM Systems
Result and Inference:
In this experiment, we carried out the implementation of the channel coding in OFDM multi-carrier modulation using LabVIEW and NI-USRP 2920. We infer that for OFDM systems to fully utilize their potential for high-data-rate communication in frequency-selective (ISI) channels, they must take advantage of the diversity inherent to frequency-selective channels, making use of channel coding and interleaving. Furthermore, it is observed that a coded OFDM system results in coding gain. Coding gain approaches a constant at a high enough SNR. This means that the gap between the coded and uncoded error curves remains approximately constant at a reasonably high SNR. Intuitively, coding gain can be interpreted as the amount of additional transmit power required to achieve a particular Pe in an uncoded system (versus a coded system).

MATLAB Experiments (8–14)
Experiment 8: Simulation of Large Scale Fading
Result and Inference:
In this experiment, we studied the effects of large-scale fading with the aid of a MATLAB simulation. From the results, we draw the following conclusions. The free-space path loss at the carrier frequency of fc = 1.5GHz for different antenna gains as the distance varies. It is obvious that the path loss increases by reducing the antenna gains. In the free-space model, the average received signal in all the other actual environments decreases with the distance between the transmitter and receiver, d, in a logarithmic manner. Furthermore, the log-distance path loss at the carrier frequency of fc = 1.5GHz shows clearly that the path loss increases with the path loss exponent n. Even if the distance between the transmitter and receiver is equal to each other, every path may have different path loss since the surrounding environments may vary with the location of the receiver in practice. Moreover, the path loss that follows the log-normal shadowing model at fc = 1.5GHz with σ = 3dB and n = 2 clearly illustrates the random effect of shadowing that is imposed on the deterministic nature of the log-distance path loss model.
Experiment 9: Study of Receive Diversity, MRC and Equal Gain Combining
Result and Inference:
In this experiment, we studied the various receive diversity techniques such as SC, MRC, and EGC. From the simulation results, we observe that MRC outperforms in terms of the achievable BER. Furthermore, we draw the following conclusions. Selection diversity is easy to implement because all that is needed is a side monitoring station and an antenna switch at the receiver. However, it is not an optimal diversity technique because it does not use all of the possible branches simultaneously. On the other hand, the Maximal ratio combines each of the M branches in a co-phased and weighted manner such that the highest achievable SNR is always available at the receiver. Furthermore, the performance of EGC is only marginally inferior to maximal ratio combining and superior to selection diversity.
Experiment 10: Study of Transmit Diversity Concepts
Result and Inference:
In this experiment, we studied the transmission strategy in the Alamouti scheme. From the simulation results, it is inferred that the transmit diversity techniques in the absence of channel knowledge at the transmitter may be designed to extract spatial diversity in systems with more than two transmit antennas. Furthermore, compared to equivalent receive diversity, the presented technique observes the advantage of having simpler, smaller, and less expensive subscriber equipment while providing an efficient remedy to the Rayleigh fading channel. Besides, Alamouti’s approach is more popular thanks to its simple formulation, efficiency, and flexible adaptability to other configurations (MISO and MIMO) and coding schemes (space-time vs. space-frequency).
Experiment 11: Simulation of MIMO System Spatial Multiplexing and Interference Cancellation
Result and Inference:
In this experiment, we studied the spatial multiplexing and interference cancellation techniques in a MIMO system. From the simulation results, we observe that the ML receiver is the best in performance, followed by the MMSE-SIC and ZF-SIC receivers, as also seen in [4]. In terms of receiver complexity, ML grows exponentially with the number of transmit antennas, while the ZF-SIC and MMSE-SIC are linear receivers combined with successive interference cancellation. Optimized ZF-SIC and MMSE-SIC algorithms for reduced complexity can be found in [5]. Simulation results comparing the three schemes for a gamut of Eb/No values are displayed. These curves allow us to gauge the diversity order obtained from the slope of the BER curve.
Experiment 12: Implementation of IEEE 802.11n Standard (PHY Layer)
Result and Inference:
In this experiment, we implemented an end-to-end simulation of the IEEE 802.11n HT link with a fading TGn channel model and additive white Gaussian noise to measure the packet error rate using MATLAB. From the simulation results, we infer that in the low SNR regime, almost all the packets received are in error. However, in a high SNR regime, a significant improvement in terms of the achievable packet error rate is noticed. Furthermore, the number of packets tested at each SNR point is controlled by two parameters: maxNumPEs and maxNumPackets. Therefore, we may use larger values than those used in our simulation for meaningful results. Besides, increasing the number of packets simulated allows the PER under different scenarios to be compared. We can also try changing the transmit encoding scheme to LDPC and compare the packet error rate.
